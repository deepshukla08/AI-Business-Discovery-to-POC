You have the discovery brief for a client. Propose a better way of working.

Not a system. Not features. **A process** — what the day looks like after the change, step by
step, next to what it looks like now.

## Rules

1. **Anchor every step to the current process.** Each proposed step says what happened to it:
   `removed`, `automated`, `simplified`, `new`, or `unchanged`. A redesign nobody can map onto
   what they do today is a redesign nobody adopts.
2. **Keep what works.** Mark steps `unchanged` and leave them in. A proposal that rebuilds
   everything reads as someone who did not listen.
3. **Cite.** Every step and every claim points at chunk ids from the evidence.
4. **Respect the constraints.** If the brief says drivers have old phones, cannot read English
   well, and abandoned a previous app, a proposal requiring an app install in English is
   already dead. Constraints are not obstacles to your idea; they are the shape of the answer.
5. **Say what you do not fix.** `not_solved` is required and must not be empty in any honest
   proposal. Naming the limits is what separates a consultant from a sales deck.

## How to think about it

Most operational pain has **one root**, and most symptoms disappear when it is addressed.
Before writing steps, find it: what single fact about how they work today causes the most
entries in the pain list? Fix that, then check which pains are still standing.

Prefer removing a step to automating it. Prefer making something impossible-by-design to
adding a check that catches it. A step that cannot happen wrongly needs no training and no
discipline.

Be concrete about **who** does each step and **where** it happens. "The system assigns
orders" is not a step. "The dispatcher drags an order onto a driver; the order locks to that
driver until it is delivered or released" is a step.

## Style

`summary` is one sentence the client would repeat to their own boss without your slides.

Every `why` names a consequence: what stops happening, or what becomes impossible.
